How Search and Rescue Teams Find Missing People

When search and rescue teams search for missing people, every second counts. This article provides information on the different planning, equipment, and methods used to locate people.

The Scale of Missing Persons Cases

Every year, over 350,000 children go missing. These adolescents are often runaways who are easily exploited, trafficked, or abused within days after they go missing. (National Child Protection Task Force, 2024) However, many of these missing people are often recovered safely after being reported. In 2024, around 91% of the missing kids reported to the National Center of Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) were found following the reports. (Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention) That then raises the question—how do search-and-rescue teams find missing people?

How Teams Plan a Search

There is a wide range of missing-person cases that search-and-rescue teams consider when starting their missions. For example, some may be injured, some may be lost but have communication, or some may be lost and have no communication. People can also fall into different categories based on their age, mental or physical conditions, and more. These all lead to different approaches the teams take in their missions, but the majority follow a similar blueprint. The person who made the report should have given authorities as much information as possible to assist in finding the missing person. It is extremely important to note that there is no waiting period before the report is filed—every second counts. (National Association For Search and Rescue, 25) While the investigation is being made, the search is ongoing. These searches are often quick and based on initial information to eliminate the most probable spots the missing person could be. A command-and-control center is set up, usually at the last known location of the missing person, and the first stage of the search is planned. Using specific, collected data, the planners will then combine that information with known routes, terrain, etc., to plan out specific areas where the missing person could be. Any information found during the search is collected and given to the center to help locate the missing person accurately. Surprisingly, many missing people are found within a quarter-mile radius of their last known point. (Wilderness Medicine Magazine, 20)

Tools and Technology on the Ground

There is also a wide range of tools used during search-and-rescue missions. For searches, specially trained search dogs may be deployed to scent-track the missing individual. Technology, such as infrared cameras with thermal imaging abilities, helicopters, and drones, can all be used during searches for longer, more difficult missions. These methods can help find missing people faster and with more accuracy under specific circumstances, as larger areas can be covered. (Wilderness Medicine Magazine, 20) Combined with large databases of how lost people behave and previous search-and-rescue missions, authorities work hard to ensure the safety of missing people so they can return home as soon as possible.
