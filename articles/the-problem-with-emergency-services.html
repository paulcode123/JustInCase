The Problem with Emergency Services

The response time to an emergency is increasing, and aid might not always be

Increasing pressure on emergency services is one of the greatest causes of slowness in responding. The number of citizens that relied on 911 services is increasing more than ever before, along with the expansion of population and community development. Nevertheless, the quantity of ambulances, firefighters, and dispatchers does not always grow at the same pace. This leaves a disparity between demand and resources. According to the National Fire Protection Association, the majority of departments in the country are getting more and more calls with fewer resources in terms of staffing and funding. This imbalance would lead to more wait times during the peak hours or huge emergencies. For example, in the case of a natural disaster or a large-scale accident, emergency teams will be overwhelmed, resulting in some of the calls being postponed or solved with limited resources. Even in the case of normal situations, factors like traffic, roads, and proximity to emergency stations can add minutes of time that are vitally important. The other critical problem is the fact that people are not ready. General beliefs are that emergency services will be there in time and will be able to take control of the situation, but the first few minutes of an emergency are usually the most important. However, in the case of the lack of early intervention, the situation of an individual can become worse until the delivery of treatment. Basic abilities such as CPR, AED, or knowledge of how to act in case of a fire will be of great help. The American Red Cross statistics demonstrate that two or even three times more lives may be saved in case of cardiac arrest due to immediate bystander intervention. However, many individuals are not trained or are not confident enough to intervene in case of an emergency. In addition to this, a lack of planning can make the situation increasingly complicated.
