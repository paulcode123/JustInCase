Emergency Preparedness for People with Disabilities

For those with physical, sensory, or cognitive disabilities, handling emergencies can become more complicated and dangerous than they already are. Read this article to learn how to stay prepared during an emergency when navigating a disability.

Emergencies alone pose as incredibly intimidating situations that occur with little to no warning. These situations alone are challenging for everyone. For those with disabilities, emergencies quickly become far scarier. To avoid being unprepared, accessible emergency planning is essential to ensure the safety of those involved.

Barriers People with Disabilities Face

People with disabilities face many barriers in emergencies. For one, those with mobility impairments may have difficulty evacuating when elevators become unavailable. Those with vision difficulties may have trouble accessing visual information, while those with auditory difficulties may struggle to access audio information. People with cognitive impairments will require clear and simple instructions to properly follow emergency procedures. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, preparing for these emergencies does not properly address the specific needs of disabled persons, leaving them vulnerable and in unsafe situations.

Building a Personal Emergency Plan

Due to this vulnerability, it is vital to create an emergency plan that prepares for individualized needs before disasters. The CDC recommends the development of emergency plans that include a week's supply of medications and medical equipment, emergency contact information, and individualized ways to receive alerts. Those with disabilities need to identify support systems of family, friends, and/or caregivers to assist in the case of an emergency.

Accessibility at the Community Level

Beyond the individual, emergency management agencies also play a vital role in accessibility. Emergency shelters must be physically accessible to all, transportation must be accommodating, and communication measures should be available in multiple accessible formats such as American Sign Language, Braille, large print, captions, and plain language. The CDC recommends the CMIST methodology, which highlights communication, maintaining health and independence, safety and support, and transportation to remain accessible to the functional needs of people with disabilities.

To conclude, emergency planning ensures that those with disabilities can properly respond to and recover from disasters. Considering the diverse needs of all before emergencies occur helps to create an inclusive emergency preparedness system in which everyone can benefit.
