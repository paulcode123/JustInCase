What a Heart Attack Really Looks Like

About 805,000 Americans experience a heart attack each year, making heart attacks one of the leading causes of death in the United States. Read this article to learn the warning signs of a heart attack to respond as quickly and efficiently as possible.

When most people think of someone having a heart attack, they usually think of someone grabbing their chest and slowly falling to the ground. That is what movies show, however, many heart attacks actually don't look that way, and because people don't realize what's happening, they wait too long to get help. Recognizing what an actual heart attack looks like and preventing any serious situations from getting worse is key to helping people.

Recognizing the Warning Signs

The most typical symptom is still chest discomfort, but it usually feels more like pressure or tightness than actual pain. The American Heart Association also lists shortness of breath, nausea, lightheadedness, and cold sweats as signals that could mean it's a heart attack. These could show up without any chest pain at all. This illustrates how someone could feel dizzy and nauseous and have no idea their heart is the problem. A lot of people usually think these symptoms are food poisoning or anxiety and just wait for it to pass, which is really terrifying to think about.

Symptoms Can Look Different for Everyone

It's also significant to know that symptoms can look pretty different depending on the person. According to the Mayo Clinic, women, older adults, and people with diabetes are more likely to have symptoms that might "seem unrelated to a heart attack," like nausea or pain in the neck or back, unlike the typical chest pressure more people usually expect. The CDC also says that women might specifically feel "extreme fatigue, dizziness, and shortness of breath" as other main symptoms they could experience. As most people can realize, these symptoms sound really similar to other issues not related with heart attack. It's so easy to blame these problems on stress or being tired, which shows how a lot of women or older adults don't even consider that something is wrong with their heart.

Act Fast: Call 911

If anything ever feels even slightly off or unrelated, call 911. The American Heart Association states that calling 911 is "almost always the fastest way to get lifesaving treatment" because EMS can start helping on the way to the hospital. Don't try to drive yourself. Don't sit there and see if it gets any better. The quicker someone gets treated, the less damage is done to the heart.
