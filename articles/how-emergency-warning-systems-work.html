How Emergency Warning Systems Work

You're going about your day when suddenly, an alert sounds on your phone. How does your phone get this alert, and what should you do? Staying safe depends on your reaction.

Why Warning Systems Matter

Imagine you're sitting in class when every phone around you suddenly vibrates with a harsh, jarring alert warning of a nearby tornado. Within seconds, everyone knows the drill– to move to a safe location. In these moments, the importance of emergency warning systems shows through life or death. Whether the danger comes from inclement weather, an AMBER alert, or a public emergency, these warning systems are made to share life-saving information quickly, allowing everyone to respond appropriately and protect themselves during emergencies.

How Alerts Reach You

Several methods are used by emergency warning systems to reach as many people as possible. Once meteorologists at the National Weather Service detect dangerous weather, warnings are then sent through FEMA's Integrated Public Alert Warning System (IPAWS). From here, the information is spread via Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA), television and radio broadcasts, NOAA Weather Radio, and outdoor warning sirens. Since alerts are sent through cell towers, the majority receive them automatically without having to download an app. Through this, emergency officials can effectively notify the public within seconds, giving them as much time as possible to react before conditions worsen.

Types of Emergency Alerts

This is just one example of an emergency alert. Some alerts warn about natural disasters, including tornadoes, flash floods, hurricanes, or wildfires, while others inform the public about missing children through AMBER alerts. During a flash flood warning, for instance, a phone alert will instruct residents to move to higher ground immediately. At the same time, sirens may also sound outdoors to warn, while televisions and radio stations interrupt regular programming to provide crucial information. Officials blast these warnings across phones, TV, and outdoor sirens for a reason: if one network goes down, another will get through. But a loud alarm doesn't do much good if you just ignore it. You have to act upon it.

What to Do When You Get an Alert

Getting the alert is only half of the battle. You actually have to know what to do next. Don't just glance at the notification and swipe it away. Read the whole thing, do exactly what it says, and keep an eye on local news or weather apps for updates. Seriously, ignoring a warning or guessing that the danger is over is a massive risk. These alert systems are incredibly good at keeping people safe, but they're useless if everyone just tunes them out. Staying safe usually comes down to two simple things: paying attention and moving fast.
